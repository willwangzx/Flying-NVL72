First-principles revision using public Starlink lessons

Design inference, not SpaceX engineering

Starlink publicly emphasizes compact launch packaging and optical networking; its Mini Laser interface provides a route for partner spacecraft. This revision applies those public ideas to a much larger compute payload. It does not claim access to SpaceX internal designs or that a Starlink bus can support this heat load. [14]

Heat sets the scale

A_required = Q_peak / [η_area (εσT_skin⁴ − q_absorbed)] For 161.6 kW, ε = 0.9, η_area = 0.9, q_absorbed = 80 W/m²: At 21°C: minimum area ≈ 594 m² before capacity margin At 26°C: minimum area ≈ 546 m² before capacity margin Selected 700 m² retains ~28% capacity margin at 26°C.

The optimization is conditional on warmer component cooling. If inlet temperature is limited to 25°C, use the 800 m² fallback preset; its hardware differs from the displayed 700 m² baseline. A small satellite outline cannot replace the radiating area required by the heat balance.

Connectivity is a service constraint

Useful average throughput equals allocated capacity × payload efficiency × usable service fraction. A lower peak-rate relay can outperform intermittent direct-ground access, but only if capacity and coverage are actually supplied. Model those terms independently.

Do not count a cloud-free space link as a guarantee of an always-available Internet path. The mesh, ground gateways, scheduling and the provider agreement remain part of the system.

Preserve the compute topology

Retain 72 GPUs, 36 CPUs, nine NVLink switch trays and a short local copper interconnect. Repackage ancillary electronics onto cold plates and replace commercial AC power shelves with protected DC distribution. The vented protective structure carries no intentional atmospheric pressure; sealed coolant circuits retain their own working pressure.

Energy and heat

Deploy the infrastructure

Four 5.5 × 50 m solar blankets and four 5 × 35 m radiator wings deploy on separate booms. Solar normals track the Sun; radiators present their emissive faces toward cold sky and keep direct solar incidence low.

Mission

600 km dawn–dusk SSO

Target inclination about 97.8°, 06:00/18:00 local-time plane; three-year design goal. Dawn–dusk reduces eclipse exposure, but the energy design uses a conservative 36-minute eclipse. Dedicated access windows and cached workloads support operation between contacts.

The first radiator factor 0.90 is an effective-area allowance for fin efficiency and obstruction; the second is infrared emissivity. The environment input is an absorbed heat flux on effective emitting area, not a temperature. Deep-space background is negligible here; Earth infrared, albedo, Sun and spacecraft mutual irradiation enter through the environment term. This is a uniform-temperature sizing model, not a nodal thermal analysis.

Charge/discharge efficiencies are each 95%. All cycle loss is conservatively assigned to the sunlit thermal peak. Excess array power is curtailed at the arrays, not dumped into the compute thermal loop. Array heating is rejected from the blankets themselves; their mutual heating contribution must stay inside the radiator environment allowance. The eclipse duration input is a stress-test setting and is not automatically derived from beta angle. The ideal zero-beta geometric maximum at 600 km is about 35.43 min; the selected 36 min is a slightly conservative energy stress case.

Only orbit period changes with altitude; radiation, drag, contact time, solar degradation and radiator geometry do not automatically change. The nominal calculator power excludes 5 kW electric propulsion; reduce rack power by at least 10 kW during burns. Fixed overhead is a sizing allocation, not a measured load curve.

Two independently isolatable 150 kW converter trains supply a regulated intermediate bus, with eight protected distribution cassettes and local point-of-load conversion. The exact bus voltage and board sequencing must match the supported compute hardware. The commercial rack’s AC power shelves are reference context only. [1] [3]

The 120 kW allocation is electrical input after the main DC–DC conversion and includes local conversion losses, compute, NVLink, NIC and onboard rack-management loads. At 96% main-converter efficiency, its input is 125 kW and its loss is 5 kW. This retains the earlier energy budget without claiming an unmeasured efficiency saving.

At 50 V, 120 kW means approximately 2,400 A in aggregate. Divide it among short local branches—about 300 A across eight equal branches—rather than routing a single low-voltage harness along the deployable wings. Each redundant 150 kW train would require roughly 3,000 A output capability at 50 V. Bus bars, connectors, contact resistance, fault energy and transient currents need detailed design.

Use vacuum-compatible insulation, isolated converters, pre-charge, branch current limiting and bonded chassis. Disable high voltage during ascent and venting until the qualified operating-pressure range is reached. All power modules conduct heat into cold plates. An independent 28 V essential bus maintains spacecraft safety when the compute assembly is off.

Array sizing and power failure behavior

Four independent wings, each 275 m², provide about 74.5 kW EOL each. The 1,100 m² calculation assumes 30% cell efficiency, 90% cell coverage, 80% combined EOL/operating-temperature factor, 97% tracking factor and 95% wiring/MPPT efficiency. These are design assumptions requiring vendor guarantees, especially cell temperature and degradation.

Baseline worst-eclipse requirement is 251.8 kW during sunlight, leaving 18.3% generation margin. Losing one solar wing reduces output to 223.5 kW; the maximum rack input with the same eclipse and 10% reserve falls to roughly 105 kW. Full 72-GPU hardware remains installed, but workload power must be capped. Safe mode isolates the rack and uses an allocated ≤8 kW essential load, with radiator flow bypass and freeze protection.

The warmer 30°C inlet is a conditional engineering target. If any component cannot meet its temperature limit, revert to the prior 25°C inlet / 800 m² design or derate the workload; do not assume supplier acceptance. The sealed coolant loop controls boiling and cavitation independently of the surrounding vacuum. Qualify joints, accumulators, cold plates, coolant chemistry, corrosion control and pump operation; do not expose liquid directly to space.

Direct cold-plate cooling does not mean immersion cooling: the electronics remain dry and coolant flows through sealed channels. For every device, verify T_j = T_fluid + P_device × R_total, including junction-to-case resistance, contact interfaces, spreaders and cold plates. A good rack-average coolant temperature cannot prove that every small component stays cool.

Two 100 kW primary heat-exchanger modules work in parallel, each with redundant pump heads and check valves. Use double-wall exchangers with monitored interspaces to provide an additional leak barrier between the ammonia and the primary coolant. The secondary system has four independent wing branches, each rated ≥60 kW, isolated by normally closed leak-control valves.

Why 700 m² of radiator?

Skin temperature: 299.15 K Emissivity: 0.90 Absorbed environment allowance: 80 W/m² Effective area factor: 0.90 Net capacity: 295.83 W per physical m² 700 m² → 207.1 kW Peak requirement with reserve: 161.6 kW Capacity / requirement − 1: 28.2%

Only one face of each panel is credited. Four wings provide 175 m² apiece; each comprises fourteen 5 × 2.5 m panels. Back faces use insulation and sun-rejection treatments. Thermally conductive facesheets and parallel fluid passages spread heat across each panel.

The environmental allowance is conditional: with solar absorptivity 0.10, direct sunlight alone can add 136 W/m² at normal incidence. A representative 80 W/m² allowance comprises 20 solar + 40 Earth IR + 20 albedo/mutual heating. Solar incidence must be nearly edge-on or shaded; radiator gimbals and orbit-dependent attitude scheduling enforce the envelope. [4] [7]

Critical bound: at 26°C and 700 m², the maximum absorbed environment compatible with 161.6 kW is about 152 W/m². At 180 W/m², full-load cooling fails. The hot-environment preset exposes this rather than hiding it in a “space is cold” assumption.

Primary: ṁ = 120 kW / (4.18 kJ/kg/K × 10 K) = 2.87 kg/s ≈ 172 L/min At 200 kPa Δp and 60% pump efficiency: P_pump ≈ 0.96 kW No air-cooling path is credited. Satellite converter and service cold plates also feed the heat-rejection system. External NH₃, 161.6 kW and 10 K rise: ṁ ≈ 3.44 kg/s using cp ≈ 4.7 kJ/kg/K ≈ 0.86 kg/s per wing

These are total flow estimates; branch balancing, cold-plate pressure drop, exact fluid properties and panel pressure loss remain to be measured. The retained 6 kW thermal-control allocation accommodates primary and secondary pumps, valves, instrumentation and additional branch losses. It is a conservative allowance pending the hydraulic design.

Microgravity provisions: fully flooded forced-circulation loops, bladder accumulators, capillary gas separators, degassed fill, bubble-tolerant startup and pump-inlet pressure monitoring. Do not depend on buoyancy, open reservoirs or gravity-fed CDUs.

Use pumped single-phase ammonia externally, following an established class of space thermal architecture, with a preliminary 2 MPa absolute operating pressure target to maintain subcooling near 33°C. Exact relief pressures and materials require pressure-system design. Route ammonia outside the inner electronics shield and isolate it from the primary coolant through double-wall exchangers. [6]

At low load, mix warm bypass flow and progressively isolate panels. Heated accumulators and cold-end sensors prevent freezing; any isolated branch needs controlled drain-back into an accumulator or validated freeze/thaw tolerance. At 8 kW safe-mode load, do not continue flowing through all 700 m². Two minutes of thermal ride-through at full power would require about 18 MJ, so shutdown on loss of circulation is immediate; no large passive heat buffer is credited.

Failure containment

A single punctured panel is isolated at the panel-pair or wing manifold. Losing one entire 175 m² wing leaves about 155.3 kW at baseline conditions, below the reserved 161.6 kW peak. The rack input must drop below roughly 115 kW just to close the scalar budget, or to about 103 kW to restore a 10% capacity reserve. A nominal 100 kW fault-mode cap is the operational target pending thermal testing. Loss of two wings requires a much larger reduction.

Pump-speed, flow, pressure, coolant conductivity, fluid inventory, temperature and leak sensors feed a separate safety controller. Flow loss first blocks new GPU work, then trips tray power if limits persist. A leaking cold-plate circuit can remove a tray from service; a complete 72-GPU domain after such a failure is not guaranteed.

Physical design and Starship accommodation

Vented frame and removable shielding

Use a 3.2 m clear-diameter, 6.0 m-long structural bay with a 3.5 m external allocation for insulation, debris shielding and harness clearance. Removable segmented panels protect the electronics, but are vented through baffled paths so they do not retain atmosphere. There is no habitable volume or gas-pressure vessel.

Retain a 1.2 × 1.5 × 2.5 m / 2,000 kg compute-assembly allocation until the custom hardware is defined. Short copper NVLink paths, connector alignment and signal integrity constrain repackaging; this is not permission to scatter GPUs arbitrarily across the satellite. Motherboards, switches, firmware and fabrics need a supported supplier integration path.

Mount cold-plated trays into a load-carrying cradle with conductive clamps, restrained connectors and independently supported manifolds. Panels and thermal blankets do not carry primary launch loads. Vent baffles and local graded shielding must limit direct debris and radiation paths without trapping ascent pressure.

No pressure-shell hoop-stress requirement for the compute bay. Pressure qualification remains necessary for coolant circuits and tanks. Provisional structural limit-load cases: 6 g axial and 2 g lateral, analysed separately 23.7 t × 6 g ≈ 1.40 MN axial interface load

Frame buckling, bolted joints, tray restraint, thermal expansion, vibration, acoustic loads and launcher coupling remain unverified. The 6 g / 2 g cases are concept assumptions, not current Starship requirements. The previous overall launch envelope is retained; removing the pressure shell does not itself validate a smaller folded-wing package.

Deployment sequence and deployed clearances
  1. Launch: rack off; coolant filled and secured; batteries isolated except essential bus; all wings and gimbals latched.
  2. Separate: verify launch-vehicle clearance, stabilize on the essential bus, acquire Sun and Earth, inspect coolant pressure, venting and structural integrity.
  3. Radiators: articulated arms move the four packs clear of the upper deck one at a time; place two roots on each ±Y side at X = ±4 m, Y = ±8 m. Extend each 35 m panel chain outward, latch booms, then enable low-flow circulation. Initial booms hold the two parallel wings apart.
  4. Solar arrays: rotate the longitudinal cassettes and deploy two wings on each ±X side, with roots at X = ±5 m, Y = ±4 m. Each 50 m wing extends outward. In the illustrated deployed plan, solar blankets occupy |Y| ≤6.75 m, while radiator panels begin at |Y| =8 m, avoiding planform overlap. Radiator wings rotate about their long Y axes; the model shows their 5 m width vertical, edge-on to the illustrative +Z Sun direction. The thermal emitting face points outward along ±X.
  5. Commission: verify independent articulation and Sun/Earth keep-out angles, then energize the protected DC converter feeds. Increase rack load in steps only after telemetry demonstrates stable coolant and individual component temperatures.
  6. Operate: slowly track array and radiator angles; fine-point communication terminals without slewing the full flexible structure. Never run the rack at full power with stowed radiators.

The static deployed span is approximately 110 × 86 m. This geometry is much larger than its launch package, but size alone does not prove structural stiffness, deployment reliability or thermal visibility. Fluid flex hoses at 13 hinges per radiator wing are a major life/pressure qualification item. Routing includes loop expansion accommodation, restrained bend radii and leak isolation; a validated hose design is still needed.

Mass, orbit control and lifetime

Mass allocation · baseline only

SubsystemkgBasis

20% growth is applied to dry mass, then propellants are added. These are parametric allocations, not supplier quotes. The mass budget does not automatically rescale with the calculator. The major uncertainty is the 700 m² radiator and deployment hardware. Concept B reassigned pressure-shell allowance to shielding and custom cold plates. Concept C removes 100 m² of radiator at the unchanged 6 kg/m² allocation: 600 kg dry, or 720 kg including 20% growth. The resulting launch allocation is 23.72 t. No additional mass or power saving is credited for replacing the communications terminals until actual Mini Laser interfaces are known.

Keep a large flexible platform controlled

Use a radiation-tolerant flight computer, dual star trackers, gyros and GNSS. Allocate four 250 N·m·s class control-moment gyros, magnetorquers for assistance and thruster unloading. This is actuator sizing intent, not a closed dynamics design; modal analysis and momentum accumulation determine final hardware.

At 4.56 μPa solar pressure, a conservative 1,800 m² projected area could experience roughly 0.008 N before reflection effects. A one-metre pressure-center offset produces torque around 0.008 N·m, adding ~48 N·m·s per orbit. Aerodynamic torque can dominate at high density. Symmetric deployment and movable pointing geometry must keep the pressure center near the center of mass. Avoid rapid whole-body slews.

Propulsion allocation

A 5 kW electric thruster system at an assumed 1,600 s specific impulse uses 500 kg xenon, giving about 334 m/s ideal Δv at the allocated mass. A separate 230 s chemical system with 2,200 kg propellant gives about 225 m/s after electric-propellant consumption, reserved mainly for controlled disposal and urgent maneuvers.

Illustrative drag, not a density forecast: ρ = 1e−12 kg/m³; Cd = 2.2; A_projected = 700 m² v ≈ 7.56 km/s; m ≈ 23.7 t Δv_drag ≈ 0.5 ρ Cd A v² / m × 1 year ≈ 58.6 m/s/year; ~175.7 m/s over 3 years Tenfold density → tenfold drag demand.

Reserve electric Δv ~250 m/s for drag and ~40 m/s for orbit/collision management; remaining ~44 m/s is contingency. High solar activity or a larger effective drag area can exhaust this allocation. Dawn–dusk illumination does not eliminate atmospheric drag.

End of life: reserve ~175 m/s chemical Δv for a controlled reentry from 600 km, plus ~25 m/s navigation/operational allowance. A 600 × 50 km osculating orbit needs about 157 m/s impulsively at apogee; finite-burn, targeting and disposal margins still require trajectory analysis. Large surviving compute-frame and propellant-tank fragments preclude assuming harmless uncontrolled reentry.

One PB buffers approximately 5.14 days of output at an 18 Gb/s mean generation rate; actual buffer life depends on the job. Provision enough cache for a multi-day service interruption.

The spacecraft-to-spacecraft link has no ground cloud path, but downstream gateways, weather diversity, mesh congestion and terrestrial routing still affect end-to-end service. An individual 2,500 km link contributes about 8.34 ms propagation; the full path includes other space links, a downlink, queues and terrestrial routing. No fixed end-to-end latency is promised.

If both optical links drop, continue only cached work that fits power, thermal and storage limits. Use S-band to command recovery; prevent cache overflow by stopping new jobs. If terminal service cannot be contracted, this architecture is not communications-ready and must revert to a separately qualified communications alternative before launch.

Keep tightly coupled GPU communication within the local NVLink domain. A 25 Gb/s external link does not turn multiple satellites into one NVL72 fabric.

08

Radiation, debris and autonomous recovery

Vacuum packaging and radiation are separate problems

Select compatible thermal-interface materials, adhesives, coatings, capacitors, connectors and pump/motor materials; screen outgassing and contamination and qualify energized hardware through hot/cold vacuum cycles. Removal of the pressure vessel is conditional on those tests, not achieved by simply removing the fans. [12] [13]

Liquid cooling and vacuum-compatible packaging do not make GB200, HBM, flash or custom converters radiation qualified. A high-inclination 600 km orbit encounters a different radiation environment from a terrestrial data center. Full-rack reliability cannot be inferred from a small computer flown on another mission.

Allocate 1,600 kg for local graded shielding around the compute volume, initially exploring approximately 10 mm aluminium plus 20 mm hydrogen-rich material over ~20 m², with remaining allowance for supports and penetration treatment. This is a mass/geometry hypothesis, not a dose guarantee; the local shield panels and debris layers add material but do not eliminate energetic particles or single-event effects.

Model the actual orbit, mission duration, geometry and solar environment, then test representative GPUs, CPU, HBM, NIC, SSD, power and BMC parts for TID, displacement damage where relevant, destructive latch-up and other single-event effects. Shielding can change secondary-particle production and must be optimized rather than blindly thickened. [9]

Fault containment without a resident technician

  • Independent radiation-tolerant controller owns pump safety, power isolation, attitude and communication recovery. A hung GPU job cannot disable spacecraft protection.
  • Current-limited tray branches and fast overcurrent trips reduce damage from latch-up where the hardware allows it. Supplier access is needed to implement protective interfaces.
  • Enable ECC and scrubbing where supported; log correctable errors, use application checksums and replicated checkpoints. ECC is not proof against all silent computation errors.
  • Checkpoint on radiation alerts or sustained error growth. Reset or isolate failed trays; expect reduced capacity and potential loss of the full 72-GPU domain.
  • Use spaced debris shielding around the vented compute bay, guarded hose routes and sectional radiator valves. Quantify debris impact risk across the entire 700 m² thermal surface.
  • Coolant pressure loss, excessive component temperature, battery fault indications or a major leak trigger compute shutdown. Batteries occupy separate ventable, thermally controlled modules. A coolant leak into vacuum can freeze, deplete inventory and contaminate optics; detect pressure/flow changes and isolate affected branches rapidly.

First article

Start with a representative compute tray, switch tray and power module in thermal vacuum. Prove every component heat path and the vacuum materials first, then build the complete 72-GPU domain. Cooling tests performed only in room air can conceal missing conductive paths.

What is not yet closed

Orbital thermal view factors, radiation lifetime, complete deployment kinematics, wing stiffness, battery ageing, contact availability, transient power, exact flight mass and present-day Starship interface.

Feasibility conclusion

The scalar energy and envelope calculations close under the stated assumptions; custom-board integration and full vacuum thermal performance are still unproven. It is a roughly 24 t orbital platform with ~100 m deployed spans, rather than a small satellite. Continuous full-rack availability remains unproven.
